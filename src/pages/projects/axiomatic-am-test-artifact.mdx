## Challenge, objectives, and scope

No single benchmark geometry can represent every additive-manufacturing process, machine, build volume, material, feature scale, orientation, and measurement system. The project therefore addressed a more durable question:

> How can a test artifact be designed systematically for a defined set of geometric-performance and measurement needs?

The work pursued five connected objectives:

1. assess form, size, orientation, position, and geometric resolution on one compact artifact;
2. expose different build orientations, feature scales, internal and external geometries, unsupported surfaces, and free-form features;
3. translate each evaluation need into a measurable feature and an inspection route;
4. preserve scanner and probe access, datum stability, and a practical on-plate inspection condition;
5. control material, build time, footprint, post-processing, support-removal effort, and avoidable digital-chain errors.

The first experiment was intentionally bounded. Repeat builds, causal separation of individual error sources, and fit-for-assembly pairs were deferred so that the available resources could focus on a general GD&T-oriented diagnostic artifact. That scope choice is important: the study was designed to reveal engineering effects, not to establish statistical process capability.

## Axis 1 — Mechanical and axiomatic design

The artifact definition contained **13 functional requirements** and **5 design constraints**. I translated these 18 inputs into **14 measurable feature groups** within an overall envelope of **73.8 × 68.85 × 16.65 mm**.

### Axiomatic-design logic

| Design step | Engineering decision | Output |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Frame the need | Start from the intended evaluation and inspection context, not from a predetermined benchmark shape | Classified functional requirements and constraints |
| Decompose | Break broad evaluation goals into elementary functions that can be solved and measured | Form, dimension, orientation, location, resolution, access, and digital-chain needs |
| Form modules | Group related requirements so that one feature can provide several useful measurands without unnecessary redundancy | Requirement modules and feature families |
| Map FRs to DPs | Select geometry, size, orientation, repetition, and location as explicit design parameters | FR–DP mapping and a compact artifact architecture |
| Preserve independence | Review interactions between feature choices, access, supports, datums, material, and build envelope | Iterated layout and controlled compromises |
| Reduce information burden | Prefer solutions that satisfy the required coverage with fewer fragile assumptions and lower manufacturing or inspection effort | Ranked concepts and a justified embodiment |

The 2018 work established the requirements-driven method; the 2020 peer-reviewed publication formalized it with Axiomatic Design concepts, including functional requirements, design parameters, design-matrix reasoning, requirement independence, and information-conscious solution selection.

### Artifact architecture

| Feature family | Examples in the artifact | Principal measurands or questions |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Planar and prismatic | Datum-bearing base, cube ladders, oriented planes | Flatness, straightness, size, angularity, parallelism, perpendicularity |
| Circular | Cylinders, holes, and coaxial hollow cylinders | Diameter, circularity, cylindricity, concentricity, and location |
| Tapered and curved | Truncated cones and hemispheres | Form, taper behavior, internal–external comparison, and unsupported geometry |
| Resolution features | Decreasing holes, bosses, slots, and polygonal features | Minimum feasible size, powder evacuation, and orientation sensitivity |
| Complex geometry | Positive and negative free-form surfaces | Reproduction of non-prismatic geometry and measurement accessibility |

The final layout balanced geometric coverage with probe clearance, scanner line of sight, powder removal, support strategy, material use, build duration, post-processing, and the need for a stable reference base.

## Axis 2 — Precision metrology

Metrology was part of the design input, not an end-of-project add-on. Feature type, orientation, access, datum strategy, and expected comparison method were considered while the artifact was being developed.

### Inspection chain

| Stage | Method | Purpose |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Global acquisition | Kreon laser scanner mounted on a CMM | Capture the manufactured surfaces while the artifact remained on the plate |
| Point-cloud preparation | Cleaning and adaptive filtering | Prepare the measured representation for nominal comparison |
| Global comparison | Point-cloud-to-CAD analysis in CATIA, checked in GOM Inspect | Visualize distributed geometric deviations against the nominal model |
| Form and orientation checks | Contact-stylus CMM with Renishaw MODUS | Quantify selected form and orientation characteristics |
| Interpretation | Group results by geometry, orientation, internal/external role, and datum relationship | Convert measurements into design and experiment lessons |

The numerical record contains **39 form and dimensional values** plus **23 orientation values**, for **62 physical measurement values** from the manufactured artifact. These are the finalized measurement outputs used in the study; the public portfolio does not distribute the raw scan or native CMM files.

### Concrete results from the manufactured artifact

| Physical observation | Measured result | Engineering reading |
| --- | ---: | --- |
| Flatness of four base corner planes | Mean **0.367 mm**; range **0.2277–0.4682 mm** | My analysis associated the dominant lower-artifact deviations with an apparent angular build-plate offset |
| Form deviation of X/Y-oriented cylinders | Mean **0.595 mm**; range **0.4224–0.7944 mm** | Cross-build cylinders were markedly less accurate than Z-oriented cylindrical surfaces |
| Form deviation of Z cylindrical surfaces | Mean **0.081 mm**; range **0.0431–0.2527 mm** | Orientation strongly influenced the observed form accuracy |
| Form deviation of unsupported cones | Mean **0.587 mm**; range **0.4686–0.6720 mm** | Visual and measured evidence both supported a support-aware design strategy |
| External-feature dimensional deviation | Mean **0.041 mm**, five measurements | External Z-oriented features formed the more accurate selected comparison group |
| Internal-feature dimensional deviation | Mean **0.173 mm**, five measurements | The mean was **4.24×** the external-feature value |
| Maximum perpendicularity error | **0.4682 mm** | The dominant case involved base-related references |
| Maximum parallelism error | **0.291 mm** | The highest value involved datum A and plane C |
| Maximum concentricity error | **0.928 mm** | Coaxial relationships exposed a major alignment deviation |

![Summary of the physical metrology results](/projects/am-test-artifact/metrology-summary.svg)

### From measurement to design action

| Evidence | Design or project implication |
| --- | --- |
| X/Y cylinders showed much higher form deviation than Z-oriented cylindrical surfaces | Treat orientation as a design variable and include deliberately controlled orientation comparisons in the next build |
| Internal features showed a 4.24× higher mean dimensional deviation than the selected external group | Review minimum internal sizes, powder evacuation, access, compensation, and the inspection strategy together |
| Unsupported cones showed large form deviations | Revisit overhang angle, supports, local heat accumulation, and post-processing access |
| Base flatness and perpendicularity were dominant cases | Separate plate alignment, base distortion, fixturing, removal, and measurement-reference effects in a controlled follow-up experiment |
| Concentricity reached 0.928 mm | Strengthen datum transfer and alignment control for coaxial feature stacks |

The experiment was diagnostic rather than statistical. Its value was to make orientation effects, unsupported-feature behavior, internal-feature difficulty, and reference/alignment issues visible in one connected design–manufacture–inspection problem.

## Axis 3 — Technical project delivery

The work was managed as a sequence of engineering decisions rather than as an isolated CAD exercise.

| Phase | Management decision | Principal output |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1. Research framing | Compare existing artifacts and replace geometry-first selection with a requirements-led method | State-of-the-art review and design criteria |
| 2. Scope and priorities | Focus the first experiment on general GD&T assessment; defer causal experiments, repeat builds, and assembly pairs | Ranked requirements and explicit exclusions |
| 3. Concept development | Group related requirements to reduce geometric redundancy and resource consumption | Requirement modules and feature families |
| 4. Embodiment design | Balance coverage, accessibility, buildability, envelope, material, supports, and post-processing | CAD model, feature architecture, and six-page dimensioned definition |
| 5. Build and inspection planning | Fix material, process parameters, on-plate inspection, laser scanning, and tactile checks | Manufacturing and inspection plan |
| 6. Verification and learning | Compare the physical artifact with nominal geometry and identify dominant deviations | Measurement tables, deviation maps, conclusions, and next actions |
| 7. Dissemination and reuse | Convert the study into durable research and engineering evidence | 2019 conference contribution, 2020 article, and controlled portfolio case study |

### Decisions and risks controlled

- **Scope:** repeatability, causal separation, and assembly tests were recorded as future work rather than implied by one build.
- **Resources:** geometry coverage was balanced against material, build time, footprint, support removal, and inspection effort.
- **Interfaces:** design, file conversion, process planning, build, scanning, tactile measurement, analysis, and publication were treated as one technical chain.
- **Evidence quality:** physical historical measurements and later synthetic software data were separated and labelled.
- **Authorship and collaboration:** I carried out the engineering work described here; the 2019 and 2020 publications retain full coauthorship and institutional attribution.
- **Release control:** the 2026 extension adds a charter, WBS, risk register, decision log, verification matrix, publication gates, version tags, and continuous integration.

## Deliverables and engineering value

The project produced more than a geometry:

- a classified set of 18 requirements and constraints;
- a feature-to-measurand architecture covering 14 feature groups;
- a compact CAD definition and six-page dimensioned drawing;
- a manufactured NC718 artifact and documented LPBF conditions;
- a combined laser-scan and tactile-CMM inspection route;
- 62 physical measurement values organized for analysis and design decisions;
- conference and peer-reviewed publications;
- a reusable 2026 parametric and traceable engineering workflow.

The main engineering outcome is a method: begin with the evaluation purpose, derive measurable functions, control interactions and resources, manufacture the resulting artifact, and use physical evidence to decide what the next design and experiment must address.

The 2026 model is **a new parametric implementation informed by the research**. It is presented separately from the original 2018 native CAD, which is not distributed in the repository. No physical artifact has been manufactured from the 2026 model. Its synthetic records test the software workflow; they are not presented as machine or part measurements.

## Interpretation boundary and next steps

The values describe **one manufactured artifact**. During the study, I cleaned and adaptively filtered the laser point cloud, mapped it against the original nominal CAD in CATIA, verified the comparison in GOM Inspect, and analyzed form and orientation results obtained by tactile CMM with Renishaw MODUS. This public portfolio presents the resulting maps and measurement values rather than distributing raw point-cloud, native CMM, calibration, or proprietary working files.

Because the experiment used one build, the deviations combine effects from CAD-to-STL conversion, manufacture, alignment, measurement, filtering, and data treatment. They diagnose the observed artifact-and-inspection chain; they do not establish statistical machine capability, calibration status, or conformity with a current standard. A further validation stage would add repeated builds, controlled orientation and support experiments, a documented uncertainty budget, on-plate versus removed-part comparison, and physical validation of a selected 2026 parametric configuration.
